<Chapter id="research" lead="What kept breaking in the field, and who I asked to find out.">

I spent the first three weeks riding along with field technicians rather than reading fleet-operator complaint logs, because the logs only recorded what a dock's sensors could see — not what a technician could see with the housing open. I shadowed six repair callouts across two operators, photographed every failed unit before it was swapped, and kept the ones I could take apart back in the studio. By the fourth callout the pattern was already obvious; the rest of the research was making sure it held.

<MethodList>
  <Method title="Ride-alongs">Six repair callouts across two operators, shadowed rather than scheduled, so I saw the failures technicians actually triage rather than the ones that make it into a ticket.</Method>
  <Method title="Teardown log">Eleven failed units, photographed and catalogued before disassembly, then again at each layer of the housing.</Method>
  <Method title="Technician interviews">Structured conversations with [X] technicians about what they check first, and what they've stopped bothering to check.</Method>
</MethodList>

<Insights>
  <Insight title="Nobody diagnoses, they swap">
    Every technician I shadowed replaced the whole unit rather than opening it, because opening it took longer than the swap and rarely told them anything useful anyway.
  </Insight>
  <Insight title="The connector fails, not the electronics">
    Nine of eleven teardowns had healthy boards behind a corroded connector — the part everyone assumed was the failure was almost never actually it.
  </Insight>
  <Insight title="Repair budget doesn't exist, replacement budget does">
    Every operator I spoke to had a swap-unit line item and nothing for diagnosis, which quietly decides what kind of product will ever get fixed instead of thrown away.
  </Insight>
</Insights>

<PullQuote attribution="Field technician" context="Ride-along, second week">
  I stopped opening them years ago. By the time I've got a screwdriver out I could've already swapped it and be at the next stop.
</PullQuote>

</Chapter>

<Chapter id="development" lead="What I built, tore up, and built again — and what each round taught me.">

Four rounds, each one testing a single question rather than the whole product at once: first whether a technician would even use an onboard diagnostic light, then whether the connector could survive a season outdoors, then whether the housing could be opened one-handed, then whether all three answers still held once assembled together.

<ol>
  <Iteration
    n="01"
    image="./images/proto-v1.jpg"
    imageAlt="[First prototype: bare connector board with three status LEDs, no housing]"
    changed="Bench-tested a three-LED status indicator wired straight to the connector, no housing yet — just to see if the failure signal was even legible to someone who wasn't me."
    learned="Technicians read it correctly nine times out of ten without being told what the colours meant, which meant the signal itself was doing the work, not a manual."
  />
  <Iteration
    n="02"
    image="./images/proto-v2.jpg"
    imageAlt="[Second prototype: connector housed in a sealed test rig for accelerated weathering]"
    changed="Moved the connector into a sealed housing and ran it through six weeks of accelerated salt-fog and thermal cycling to chase the corrosion failure directly."
    learned="The seal held, but the fastener holding it didn't — a standard screw corroded before the connector ever did, which sent me looking for a captive, corrosion-proof fastener instead."
  />
  <Iteration
    n="03"
    image="./images/proto-v3.jpg"
    imageAlt="[Third prototype: full housing with a single captive quarter-turn fastener]"
    changed="Replaced the screw with a captive quarter-turn fastener and timed how long it took a technician to open, diagnose and close the unit one-handed, gloves on."
    learned="Under [X] seconds average, down from the four-plus minutes a full swap took — but two of five testers still reached for a tool out of habit before realising they didn't need one."
  />
</ol>

<Chapter id="delivery" lead="What shipped, and what it does for the technician holding it.">

The final dock is three parts: a weatherproof housing, the diagnostic connector board with its three-state light, and the quarter-turn fastener that ties them together without a tool. A technician sees the light from the street, knows before they've touched it which of three problems they're looking at, and can confirm or rule it out in under [X] seconds.
